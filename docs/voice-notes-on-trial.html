Voice notes on trial: a verdict can't settle a clash of norms

A Guardian column asks readers whether a partner's long voice notes are guilty. With Duncan Sabien's ideas, a better question appears: what does each message mean to each person?

  • When two people disagree about how to talk to each other, asking which of them is guilty means picking one person's habits as the rule. Ask instead what each message means to each of them, and what arrangement would cost both of them least.
  • A verdict comes with advice but not with reasons both people can check. Saying "this is how it works for me", and agreeing when to use which channel, gives each of them something to do tomorrow.
  • The common mistake: assuming a message has one true meaning that a fair jury could find. A heart emoji, a curt text or an eight-minute voice note can mean one thing to the sender and another to the reader.

The case: ten minutes of voice note

Every week, the Guardian's You be the judge column puts a couple's disagreement on trial. One partner prosecutes, the other defends, five readers give verdicts, and everyone else votes in a poll. On 29 October 2026 the charge was voice notes.The interviews are by Georgina Lawton. The column changes the couple's names.

Ayo and Elena have been together a year. They live apart and he travels for work. Both say they get on well in person; the trouble is the phone.

Ayo's case. He calls himself "a simple man" and would rather talk on a call, where he can take things in as they come. Elena sends a lot of voice notes, some for questions a text would answer. By the end of a long one he has forgotten the start. On work trips he sometimes forgets to reply at all. He thinks she reads too much into texts, and calls the habit unhealthy. When he's blunt she assumes he's angry; he says he's just "a bit less smiley on text". Her longest note ran twelve minutes. Once he answered a note with a heart, because he was on the move, and asked for a call two days later. She was annoyed.

Elena's case. She sometimes thinks "simple man" is code for a bad communicator. Because they live apart, a voice note sent while she walks or cooks gives him "a window into the life you're leading". A call needs both of them free at once; a note doesn't. She denies analysing his full stops. He is blunt over text, she says, and then surprised when she wonders whether he's annoyed. Her request is small: don't answer eight minutes with one line, and don't reply to the first thing she said while missing something important later on. She adds that he sometimes calls when she's busy and then complains that she doesn't pick up. The twelve-minute note was never repeated, "because he ignored it".

Predict: of the five reader-jurors, how many side mainly with Ayo? None or one, two or three, or four or five? Pick one before you open this.

Three. Nicola, Kitty and Margie find mainly against Elena. Daniel finds for her. Bryony splits it: Ayo could show more interest, and Elena could send fewer long notes.

If you said four or five, you probably think a twelve-minute note is plainly too long, and Kitty agrees with you. If you said none or one, you probably think a partner sharing her day deserves a hearing whatever the medium, which is Daniel's reason. Neither guess is unreasonable, and that is what the rest of this page is about. "Who is guilty?" has an answer only once you've decided what normal looks like, and the jurors decided differently.

A first try: put it to a jury

The column's method is the obvious one, so let's take it seriously before we criticise it. When two people disagree about what's reasonable, ask some outsiders. If most of them think twelve-minute voice notes are too much, maybe they are.

Now look at the reasons rather than the verdicts. Margie's reason is her own taste. Nicola's is Ayo's taste, treated as the rule. Kitty's starts from a point where twelve minutes is plainly outrageous; Daniel's from a point where any sharing is welcome. Four of the five measure the couple against an idea of normal that they don't argue for, and the four ideas differ. Bryony is the exception: she proposes a trade, which is where this page ends up too.

That's the crack in the jury method. Each verdict comes with advice, but the advice rests on the juror's own idea of normal, so Ayo and Elena can't check it against anything they share. Whatever replaces the jury has to start from what each message means to these two people.

The crack, named: a message has no single meaning

Duncan Sabien has an essay on exactly this failure, and its example is a text message.

"There is not, in fact, a single clear and obvious and unambiguous meaning to a text reply that is just the letter 'K.'"

Duncan Sabien, Single Answers Make You Stupid

You can get partway, the essay says, with clumsy proxies: check with ten people you know, and find that six read the "K" the same way. That is a poll. The same essay tells a story about what a poll's answer is worth. The US Air Force measured thousands of pilots to build a cockpit for the average one. When someone checked how many pilots were within 30% of the average on all ten of the most important measurements, the answer was none. The fix was adjustable seats.The story is from the essay; this page doesn't check its history. The essay also grants that when you're forced to pick one option, choosing what suits the median person is sensible. A newspaper poll is forced to return one number; Ayo and Elena aren't.

Put those together and we can see what the column built. Its jury is the ten-people proxy. Its verdict is a fixed seat: it decides the shape of the right partner and asks whoever doesn't fit to change shape. The simulated advisor makes the same link.

That proxy is the jury. Margie dislikes voice notes herself, so Elena is guilty. Nicola tells her to save the "mini podcasts" for friends. That's the cockpit built for the average pilot, and nobody actually sits in it.

Everyone stands on a different zero

A second essay names what each juror is doing when they pick a normal. In Setting the Zero Point, Duncan's example is a drop of five points. Five points from 87 to 82 is a dip; from 2 to −3 it's a slide from good to bad. The change is the same, but where you put zero decides the story. Choosing zero is a move in an argument, usually made without saying so. Here is the same move with the couple's messages.

Each of them is right about their own line. A long note does cost Ayo more than a call. A one-line answer to eight minutes does tell Elena her news didn't land. Both give good reasons about their own costs. What neither defends is why their line should be the one the other is measured on.

Where the meanings go wrong: mindreading in both directions

If a message has no single meaning, each reader supplies one, and that's where the couple's specific complaints come from. Duncan's essay Mindreading Fails Both Ways separates two ways it goes wrong. You can expect to be read: assume the other person will understand what you never said. Or you can believe you can read them, and treat your interpretation as what they meant.

"…they believe they can read my mind, and are interpreting me as sending secret subtle messages that I am not, in fact, sending."

Duncan Sabien, Mindreading Fails Both Ways
Predict: Ayo answered a voice note with a heart and asked for a call two days later. From the column alone, can you tell what he meant the heart to say? Yes or no? Commit before you open this.

No. All he tells us is that he was on the move. Whether the heart meant "got it, I'll call", "lovely" or "can't talk", the column doesn't say, and neither, apparently, did he. We also can't tell whether Elena was annoyed by the heart or by the two-day wait; Kitty, for one, faults the wait.

If you said yes, notice where the meaning came from: you supplied it, as Elena had to. That is how expecting to be read works. The sender knows the context (busy, on a train, will call later). The reader gets a heart.

The pattern is evenhanded. Each of them makes at least one claim about what's going on inside the other. "Unhealthy" is a diagnosis of Elena's mind; "code for a bad communicator" is a guess at Ayo's motives, offered more tentatively. Neither is something the speaker observed. And each may expect a signal of their own to carry more than it does.

One row is different, and it matters later. The long voice notes aren't a misreading. They really do cost Ayo more than they cost Elena, and both of them say so. So the dispute has two parts. The mindreading can be fixed by saying more. The cost difference can't; it needs a trade.

The fix: say it as your culture, not as the law

So the fix needs two properties. It has to stop treating either person's normal as the standard, and it has to give each of them words for their costs that the other can act on. Duncan's essay In My Culture offers a phrase with both. You preface a norm with "in my culture", and so describe how things work for you without legislating for anyone else. Let's call a sentence like that a culture statement.

"…sharing something about yourself is distinct from making a request of others…"

Duncan Sabien, In My Culture

Both of them already say true things about themselves. "I just hate voice notes" is a fact about Ayo; Elena's wish that he not answer eight minutes with one line is a fair, specific request. The column turns both into charges. As culture statements they become information: "in my culture, a long note is hard work to take in", and "in my culture, a one-line answer to a long message means you didn't listen".

Does that mean nobody can ever be in the wrong? The essay says no. When Alex does something and Bailey gets angry, it says, "the fix could be on either side", and it lists what decides it: what actually happened, how reasonable the reaction was, and whether a boundary was crossed that both had acknowledged, or that had been "previously-communicated-and-agreed-upon". That last test is the telling one here. Ayo and Elena have never agreed a norm for voice notes. There was nothing for either of them to break, which is why a verdict is premature rather than impossible.

When two people do negotiate, the essay lists the outcomes: one side's norms, the other's, a compromise, or "a set of triggers for distinguishing when to do A versus when to do B". A trigger, in that sense, is an agreed rule for when to do which: here, when a voice note is the right channel, when a text is, and when a call is.

But the essay adds something easy to skip. More often there's no negotiation. Each side now knows how its actions land, and that knowledge doesn't compel anyone to change. That limits Elena's request. Between acquaintances, "now you know" would be the end of it. Whether a relationship is a place where it isn't, where each owes the other a negotiation, is the question we come back to at the end.

A message can come with a bill

The cost difference needs one more idea. In Obligated to Respond, Duncan argues that a message can create an obligation to answer, and that the sender should own that obligation rather than create it and then deny it. The essay is about comments posted in public, where a reply that never comes is seen by onlookers, so we're borrowing its idea of cost, not its setting. Even borrowed, it gives Ayo a way to state his complaint without insulting Elena.

"…it's not your questions are bad, it's your questions are costly, and I don't have the spare resources to pay the costs; I'd like to not keep receiving bills and invoices from you…"

Duncan Sabien, Obligated to Respond (the essay's emphasis removed)

The essay's practical suggestion is to let the pressure off in the message itself, with an opener like "please feel free to skip over this if it's too time-consuming". A voice note can do the same: "no reply needed, I'm just on my walk". The other half is this page's addition: a note that does need an answer can say so first, so Ayo knows which kind he's holding.

What each of them could say next

Put the pieces together and you get statements instead of verdicts. The simulated advisor drafted one for each of them to a pattern this page asked for: a culture statement, the speaker's own cost, one retracted guess about the other's mind, one thing the speaker owns, and a proposal with a trigger. The pattern is ours, not the essays'.

Simulated Duncan, drafting for Ayo · not Duncan's or Ayo's words

"In my culture, a long voice note is a bill I can't pay on the move. By minute three I've lost minute one. That's my cost, not a flaw in your notes. I said you read texts in a way that isn't healthy. That was a guess about what's going on inside you, and I take it back. And on work trips I forget to reply at all. That's on me. A proposal: from now on, a heart means 'got it, can't engage yet.' And within a day, you get a real reply to whatever you flag."

Simulated Duncan, drafting for Elena · not Duncan's or Elena's words

"In my culture, voice notes are how I let you into my day. When eight minutes gets a one-line reply, it lands as 'you didn't listen.' That's my cost. I said 'simple man' was code for 'bad communicator.' That was a guess about what's going on inside you, and I take it back. And I send voice notes for quick questions that could be texts. That's on me. A proposal: anything I need answered, I'll open with 'one thing I need from you.' And from now on, quick logistics go by text."

Let's check the scripts against the table of signals. "Unhealthy" and "code for" are withdrawn, so the two read-their-mind rows go. The heart gets a meaning both can know, and "one thing I need from you" makes sure the important part of a note is flagged, so the two expect-to-be-read rows go too. The row that was never a misreading, the difference in cost, is handled by trade: she moves logistics to text and leads with what she needs; he commits to a reply within a day.

One row is left open: Ayo's blunt texts. An earlier draft from the advisor had a trigger for it too: when a text reads blunt, Elena asks, "Annoyed, or just texting?" Nothing in any of this needed a verdict.

What this reading can't tell you

  • We have two edited statements, not two people. The column gives each partner a few hundred words, with names changed. Everything above is about Ayo and Elena as the column presents them, not their actual psychology or relationship.
  • The essays are about other situations. None of them is about voice notes or a couple's dispute like this one. Obligated to Respond is about public comments, and its costs depend on onlookers; a private channel has none (though the column, by publishing who failed to reply to whom, supplies some). And one step is an extrapolation. In My Culture says you can leave any conversation "unless you've made a specific commitment to the contrary", and its example of owing nothing is closure after a break-up. Whether an ongoing relationship is such a commitment, so that partners owe each other more attention, and a negotiation, than strangers do, is the real question under the column. The essay doesn't settle it.
  • The commentary is simulated. The dashed passages come from a language model working from Duncan's published writing. The quotations with solid bars are his, and each links to its source; everything else is this page's reading.

Your turn

Here's a check you can try on yourself, aimed at the step this page cares about: turning a verdict into a culture statement and a trigger.

Optional exercise: a colleague sends long, detailed chat messages; you'd rather have a five-minute call. You're tempted to say "Your messages are way too long." Rewrite it as a culture statement plus a trigger. Draft one before opening this.

One version: "In my culture, anything longer than a screen is easier for me as a quick call. It's not that your messages are bad, they just cost me more to read than to hear. If it needs a decision, put the question in the first line, and I'll answer that day."

Check yours for three things. Does it describe you rather than rule on them? Does it name the cost? Does it give a trigger they can follow without guessing? A rewrite that still says "too long" is still a verdict. If yours only describes you, it's half done: the colleague learns your preference but not what to do differently.

So, is Ayo right?

Back to the poll. Ayo is right that long voice notes cost him more than they cost Elena. Elena is right that a one-line answer to eight minutes of her day tells her he didn't listen. Each is wrong where they guess at the other's mind. And the question the column asks, guilty or innocent, comes too early. There's no agreed norm yet for either of them to have broken, so any verdict just picks whose normal counts. The useful questions are what each message means to each of them, and which arrangement costs both of them least.

The cue to take away: when you catch yourself asking who is in the wrong about how someone communicates, check whether you're standing on different zeros. If you are, a verdict won't help either of you. Say what your culture is, name what it costs you, and agree on when to do which.
