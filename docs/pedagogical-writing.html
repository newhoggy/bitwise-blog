Pedagogical writing: so the reader could have invented it

Pedagogical writing is writing whose job is to teach: the reader should leave able to use an idea, not just able to say they have read it. Clarity is not enough for that. Here is what else it takes, built up from one clear paragraph that fails.

  • Order an idea so the reader could have invented it: make them need it, try the obvious answer, show exactly where that answer breaks, then give the fix, and only then its name. Then ask a question that someone still holding the wrong answer would get wrong.
  • It matters because clarity is not learning. In one physics study, a clear, correct video raised students' confidence just as much as a video that taught them twice as much.
  • The common mistake is writing in the order you now understand the idea, answer first and reason after. That hides the question the answer was for, and leaves the reader's wrong model untouched.

Already know the loop? The method in five lines is in the last section, and a routine for your next draft is in Your turn.

Clear, correct, and nothing learned

A first-year student answers a physics question, watches a seven-minute video that explains the answer, and answers it wrong again, more confident than before.

The question is about a book sliding down a rough ramp at constant speed: is the force pulling it down the ramp greater than the friction holding it back, less, or equal? She picks "greater", reasoning that something has to win for the book to keep moving. The right answer is "equal": at constant speed the forces balance. Then she watches a video that explains Newton's first and second laws with animations and live demonstrations, including a book pushed across a table at constant speed. Asked again, she answers "greater" again, and raises her confidence from 5 to 6 out of 7 "because she believes she better understands the concepts after watching the video".

She is the opening example of a paper by Derek Muller and Manjula Sharma at the University of Sydney, drawn from their studies of more than a thousand physics students over three years.They introduce her as "Imagine a first year student", so she is a typical case from their data, not one named person. Muller later started the Veritasium channel. Nothing was wrong with the video. In their words, "the sentences used to describe the phenomena are not long or convoluted, nor does the presenter seem to struggle with making the concepts plain." They compared that clear lecture with other versions of the same lesson. One, the Dialogue, was a scripted conversation in which a student says the common wrong ideas out loud and a tutor takes them apart. Another, the Refutation, had a single narrator voice and correct the same wrong ideas.

Guess before you open this: which raised the students' confidence more, the clear lecture or the Dialogue that voiced their wrong ideas? The lecture, the Dialogue, or about the same?

About the same. In the 2006 study every version raised students' confidence by 0.6 to 0.7 points on average, on the seven-point scale, with no significant difference between them. Learning was another matter: students who watched the Dialogue or the Refutation gained on average twice as much as those who watched the clear lecture.

If you picked the lecture, you had a reason: it is the more polished explanation, and polish feels like teaching. If you picked the Dialogue, you expected confidence to follow learning. It doesn't. Confidence tracks how smoothly the material went down, and a clear video goes down smoothly whether or not it changes anything. A later experiment found the same from the other side: viewers of a clear video that taught misconceptions felt just as sure as viewers of a correct one, and came away with "more misconceptions and fewer scientifically correct conceptions" (Kulgemeyer and Wittwer, 2022).

That gap between feeling taught and being taught is what this page is about. Clarity is necessary, but the ramp shows it is not sufficient: the reader also has to want the idea, meet their own wrong answer, and see where it breaks. So the question for this page is concrete. Take a clear, correct paragraph that a newcomer reads, feels good about, and cannot use. What do you change, and how do you find out whether it worked?

Here is the route. The next two sections take one such paragraph, from Wikipedia, and find exactly where it fails a reader. Then we rebuild it in four steps, which I'll call beats, and switch the beats off one at a time to see what each one does. That is the core. After it come the same idea at the scale of a whole page, the steps you can't make guessable, how to check a reader, and the same order inside one sentence. It ends with a routine for your next draft.

A first try: just say it clearly

The obvious way to explain something is to say it clearly: state the rule, give the reason, name it, and get every detail right.

This is how most of us write, and it rests on a model of reading worth stating in full. The reader takes in each sentence. If every sentence is true and clear and follows from the one before, the reader ends up holding what the writer holds. On this model, every failure to teach is a failure of clarity: a long sentence, an undefined term, a skipped step. So the cure is always more editing for clarity.

Here is a paragraph that passes that test. It is from Wikipedia's article on the dynamic array, the growable array behind Rust's Vec, Java's ArrayList and Python's list. I chose it because it is good: short, true, and in a sensible order.

If you already know why arrays double, this reads well, and that turns out to be part of the problem. One fact the paragraph takes for granted: a resize allocates a new buffer and copies every item across. With that, here is a question the paragraph should let a newcomer answer. Commit to a guess before you open it; a guess is enough.

A colleague suggests growing by a fixed 1,000 slots instead of doubling: less wasted space, and still "a large amount". Starting empty, a million appends copy roughly how many items in total? About 1 million, about 500 million, or about 500 billion?

About 500 million, nearly 500 times what doubling copies. A copy happens every 1,000 appends and moves everything so far: 1,000 + 2,000 + … + 999,000, which is 1,000 × (1 + 2 + … + 999) = 499,500,000. Doubling copies only at 1, 2, 4, … items, up to 524,288 (219), 1,048,575 in all: about one copy per item.

"About 1 million" is the answer the paragraph's first sentence teaches: "resize by a large amount", and 1,000 is large. "About 500 billion" is the count for growing by one slot at a time, 1 + 2 + … + 999,999 items. That sum is about 999,999² ÷ 2, so it grows with the square of the number of appends. A step of 1,000 divides it by 1,000, and that is all it does: the total still grows with the square. Ten million appends would cost 100 times as much, not 10 times. Doubling is cheap because its step grows with the array.

Where clear writing cracks

If you answered "about 1 million", the paragraph taught you exactly what it says first. The idea that rules out 1,000 is in it, but in the one place a reader is least likely to take it from.

Let's look at where each part sits, and at what a reader takes from it.

  • The answer comes before the question. The first sentence gives the rule, "resize by a large amount, such as doubling", for a problem stated only in the abstract: "the cost of resizing many times". The reader never sees that cost, so they have nothing to measure "large" against.
  • The wrong answer is never tried. The natural first attempt, growing by a fixed step, never appears, so it is never broken. Worse, the first sentence half-endorses it, since 1,000 slots is "a large amount". The reader's own model leaves the paragraph exactly as it came in, as the ramp student's did.
  • The idea is buried. What makes doubling work, "any constant proportion", sits inside the subject of the last sentence, among symbols, while the end of the sentence, where readers put their weight (the section on sentences says why), goes to a caveat in brackets. It is the one phrase that separates the right model from the wrong one. Remember the word proportion: we will come back to where it should have gone.

None of these is a failure of clarity: each sentence is clear. The failure is the order. The paragraph runs in the order its author holds the idea, rule first and reason after, which is the reverse of the order a newcomer could build it in. Grant Sanderson, who makes the 3Blue1Brown maths videos on YouTube and has thought harder than most about why explanations work, gives the same diagnosis as advice: "try very hard to structure your explanation to go from the concrete to the abstract. I think almost always when you understand something the natural inclination is to go the other way around."

That inclination has a name, the curse of knowledge: once you know something, it is hard to imagine not knowing it. Steven Pinker calls it "the single best explanation I know of why good people write bad prose." It is also why the paragraph may have read well to you. If you already hold the model, every gap fills itself in, so the gaps are invisible to the one person who could close them.

So the crack points at what the fix must do, and better sentences are not it. The reader has to hold the question before the answer, and their own wrong answer before the right one, so that by the time the rule arrives they can nearly guess it.

The fix: the question before the answer

This is the key step of the page. We rebuild the paragraph so that each part answers a question the part before it raised, and the rule arrives when you can nearly guess it.

Both orders at once. Wikipedia's paragraph is on top, the rewrite underneath, and each arrow shows where a part of the original ended up. The steps take the rewrite one beat at a time.

Wikipedia's order: rule, reason, name. Every sentence is true, and each follows from the last. Nothing in it is a question, and nothing in it is wrong.

Two beats with no counterpart. A question with a stake (a million appends: how much copying?) and the answer a newcomer is most likely to give: grow by a fixed 1,000 slots. It is labelled as a first try, so a skimmer doesn't keep it.

The crack counts what the attempt costs, 500 million copies, and finds the waste: every resize copies the early items again. So the room added has to grow with the array. That is Wikipedia's "any constant proportion", promoted from a clause to the conclusion of an argument.

Only now the rule: double it. It moves from first to fourth, where the reader can nearly guess it, because the crack has said what it must do.

The name is last in both. Wikipedia gets this right. In the rewrite the name labels counts the reader has just seen: a million appends, about a million copies.

A check the reader can fail. Growing by 1.5× is a near-twin of the 1,000-slot question: same idea, different surface. A reader who took away "double" rather than "in proportion" calls it quadratic, and finds out they were wrong.

The figure has six boxes, but only four beats. The name belongs to the fix, which ends on it, and the check comes after the explanation rather than inside it; it gets a section of its own. I'll call the four beats together the core loop, because on a teaching page every idea goes round it once:

  1. Need. Make the reader want the idea, with a puzzle, a goal or a failure, and put numbers in it: a million appends, how much copying?
  2. Naive attempt. Try the approach your reader would try, concretely, and label it as a first try where it appears, so a skimmer doesn't leave with it: a fixed step of 1,000 slots.
  3. Crack. Show where it breaks, and exactly where: which case, which term. Phrase the break as the property any fix must have: every resize copies the early items again, so the room added must grow with the array.
  4. Fix, then name. Give the fix in the words of that property, then its technical name with its plain meaning: double it; that is amortised O(1).

Each beat does a different job. The need is what keeps a reader reading; Sanderson argues that education "is not rate limited by the clarity of explanation, it's rate limited by motivation." The naive attempt and the crack do what Muller's Dialogue added to the clear lecture: the reader's wrong model is said out loud, as a mechanism, then broken on a case. On the ramp, that addition doubled the learning gains. And the name comes last because, as Sanderson says of notation in a good explanation, "it arrives only once it's articulating something that already exists at least loosely in the viewer's mind."

A caution about the word "invented". People left to rediscover an idea on their own learn less than people who are told it: a meta-analysis of 580 comparisons found unassisted discovery behind explicit instruction (d = −0.38), while discovery with scaffolding, worked examples and feedback came out ahead (d = +0.30).d is an effect size: the gap between two groups' average scores, in standard deviations. 0.3 is a modest but real difference. Alfieri and colleagues, 2011. The loop is meant to supply that guidance. The crack does the scaffolding: by naming the property the fix must have, it narrows the fix down to one guess, so the reader invents it only in that guided sense.

What each beat does

"Each beat matters" is easy to nod along to. Let's test it instead: switch a beat off and read what is left.

Below is the rewrite in full, each paragraph tagged with its beat. Read it once as it stands. Then switch off the Crack and read the fix again; the note under the text says what a newcomer would now be missing. The other switches work the same way, with two twists: Name last off moves the name to the top, and with the naive attempt off the crack goes too, since there is nothing left to break. Rule and name only strips the text back to Wikipedia's shape.

Notice what happens with the crack off. "Double it" is still true and still clear, but now it is one choice among many, and nothing in the text rules out the 1,000-slot step it just proposed. You are back where the Wikipedia paragraph left you. Notice too that the crack says 500 million copies, not "O(n²)". Sanderson, explaining probabilities as counts: "somehow phrases like 40 out of 100 kick our intuitions into gear much more effectively than 40%, much less 0.4." The notation O(1) waits until you have seen the counts it summarises.

Why a fixed step of 1,000, and not the even worse step of one slot (about 500 billion copies)? Because a newcomer doesn't believe in one slot; they believe in big chunks. The naive attempt has to be the wrong model your reader actually holds, or breaking it changes nothing in their head.

The loop costs words: Wikipedia's 74 (without its code) become about 300 here. For a reader who has to use the idea, that is the trade worth making; when it isn't comes later.

If you stop here, you have the core of pedagogical writing: give every idea a need, a naive attempt, a crack that says what the fix must do, the fix, and then the name. For now, treat a page as a single loop. A real page holds several ideas, and joining their loops is the next problem. Everything after this section makes the loop work at other scales: a page, a step you can't make guessable, a reader you have to check, a sentence.

But a page holds many ideas: a ladder

So far, one idea and one loop. A real page has several, and how we join their loops decides whether the page reads as a lesson or as a list.

The rewrite already shows the join. Doubling has a flaw of its own: right after a resize, up to half the new buffer is empty. That flaw is the next loop's need. Its fix is a smaller factor, such as Java's 1.5 or CPython's roughly 1.125 (Wikipedia's article has a table of them), which trades more copies for less empty space while keeping the room growing with the array. And that fix has its own flaw: every resize still copies the whole array in one go, a pause that a program with a latency budget notices, and the need for a further loop. Each fix leaves a problem, and each problem starts the next loop, so the joins between loops are always "but" or "therefore", never "and then". Nicky Case borrowed this rule for interactive explanations from Matt Stone and Trey Parker, who write stories not as "this happens, and then that happens", but as "this happens, THEREFORE that happens, BUT that happens, THEREFORE this happens".

A page in this blog's series on DEFLATE, the compression inside gzip and PNG, shows a whole ladder. You don't need to know DEFLATE to follow its shape. A compressor that has invented its own short codes for a stretch of data must first tell the decoder what they are. It does that by sending a list of 316 small numbers, one for every symbol the format allows, and most of them are 0 because most symbols never occur. The question is how to send that list cheaply. Three habits keep your ladder climbable. One new idea per rung: a section that needs a second mechanism is two sections. Measure every rung on one benchmark, as the bits above are, so the gap that remains is visibly the next need. And raise the next problem before the reader does. Just before the ladder above, that page swaps a description naming only the 11 symbols its worked example used for a list with an entry for all 316. It objects to its own move straight away: "You might object that we have swapped 11 symbol names for something worse: a length for every symbol a block could use, 316 of them, used or not. You would be right, and squeezing that list is the rest of this page." A reader who was about to object learns that the writer saw it too, and why to keep reading.

Not every attempt deserves a loop of its own. A dead end that teaches nothing gets one sentence; show in full only the attempt whose crack points somewhere.

When a step can't be guessed: promises

But not every rung can be made guessable yet. You may need a simplification for now, a "why" that only makes sense later, or a detail that would derail the story. The loop doesn't cover these steps. A promise does.

A promise is any place where you ask the reader to accept something on credit. Promises are fine. Unflagged or unpaid ones lose readers, because the reader can't tell a temporary simplification from the truth, and a skimmer keeps the simplification. Three moves handle them:

  • Flag it where you make it, with its size. Sanderson, simplifying a quantum algorithm: "You should know there is that added bit of complexity that I'm postponing, but this is 90% of the right idea." The reader now knows what kind of claim they are holding.
  • Pay it off by name. Come back to the claim, quote it, correct it, and say what the simple version would have got wrong. At its third try, the DEFLATE page writes each run symbol (a short code standing for a run of zeros) "for now … as a plain 5-bit number", and the next section opens on exactly that cost: "Step 3 still spends 5 bits on every run symbol, yet they are far from equally common."
  • Defer out loud. Name the question the reader is about to ask, say where it is answered (or that it isn't), and give them the one fact they need to carry on.

Claims need the same honesty. For each statement the page doesn't show, say where it stands: shown later, cited, measured, or only asserted. And treat every "obviously" as a claim with no status: either show it, or say where it stands.

I have made promises on this page too. "For now, treat a page as a single loop" was paid off in the last section. The word proportion is still owed; it is paid in the section on sentences.

Smooth reading isn't learning: make the reader commit

Even a perfect loop can be read without being learned, which is exactly what happened on the ramp. The only way you or the reader can find out is a question they can get wrong.

You have answered two on this page, and that was deliberate. A meta-analysis of questions asked before a lesson, with its methods registered before the analysis, found that they improved learning of the material they asked about (g = 0.54) but barely touched the rest (g = 0.04). They helped far more when people guessed an answer (g = 0.65) than when they only read the question (g = 0.22).g is the same kind of effect size as d. St. Hilaire, Chan and Ahn, 2024. So ask for a commitment, an option, a number or a shape, and say that a guess is enough. Aim it at the key step, because the benefit stays where the question points.

The harder part is choosing which question to ask. A question teaches only if the reader's likely wrong model gives a different answer from the right one. Here are two questions we could have asked about the Wikipedia paragraph:

Is doubling better than adding one slot at a time?

Both models answer yes.

  • A reader who thinks "grow by a large amount" gets it right
  • Says nothing about proportion

Grow by 1,000 slots: about how many copies?

The models disagree.

  • "A large amount" predicts about 1 million; "proportion" predicts about 500 million
  • A wrong guess tells the reader which model they hold

Build each wrong option from one misconception, and in the reveal say why each was a reasonable guess before correcting it, as the 1,000-slot reveal did. Then state the insight, not only the number, so a reader who was right for the wrong reason finds out.

When a reader meets a mechanism more than once, fade the support: work the first case fully, leave the last step of the second as a prediction, and give the third to the reader. This page does that with the loop itself. Wikipedia's paragraph was worked through for you, the DEFLATE ladder came with its cracks labelled, and the exercise is yours.

The same order inside a sentence

The loop puts what the reader already holds before what is new. A good sentence does the same, on a smaller scale.

George Gopen and Judith Swan found that readers expect a sentence to open with old information that links back, and to end on the new information it exists to deliver. They call the end of a sentence its stress position, because readers give the most weight to whatever comes just before the full stop. Their rule: "Put in the topic position the old information that links backward; put in the stress position the new information you want the reader to emphasize."

Now we can pay off proportion. Wikipedia's last sentence puts that word near the start, inside the subject, and gives its stress position to a caveat in brackets. In both versions below, the load-bearing word and the end of each sentence are marked:

As written“Expanding the array by any constant proportion a ensures that inserting n elements takes O(n) time overall, meaning that each insertion takes amortized constant time (so long as any given allocation takes O(1) time).”

Stress where it belongs“Inserting n elements then takes O(n) time overall, amortised constant time per insertion. What makes that true is that the array grows by a constant proportion, not by a constant amount.”

The second version adds no facts. It reorders them so that each sentence ends on its point, and the last one ends on the contrast a newcomer is most likely to miss: proportion, not amount. That is the 1,000-slot question, answered in the stress position. (The caveat about allocation is true but secondary; it can go in its own sentence or a footnote.)

Two more habits come from the same idea:

  • Keep one story per paragraph. List the first few words of each sentence. They should name the same character, or follow a chain in which each sentence starts where the last one ended. If they read "The array… Copies… A factor… Memory…", the paragraph has no subject, and the reader has to build the links alone.
  • One name per thing. A new word reads as a new thing. If "buffer", "allocation" and "storage" all mean the same memory, the reader counts three things. Joseph Williams, in his textbook Style, warns against this "elegant variation". It is why this page calls the beats need, naive attempt, crack and fix every time.

Figures and interactives earn their place

Order fixes most of what goes wrong in prose. But some things no ordering of sentences can fix: a reader who has to hold too much in their head, or see something words can't show. That is where a figure earns its place.

So write the prose first, find where a reader would have to juggle too much, and add a figure only there. This page's two main figures each answer one such failure. The two orders are drawn one above the other, because comparing a paragraph with its rewrite from memory is a memory test. Amit Patel, who makes the interactive explanations at Red Blob Games, puts it as a rule: "It's often better to show all the variants than to ask the reader to interact with the diagram to see the variants." The beat switches are interactive for the opposite reason: there are more variants (one per switch, and their combinations) than a page can show, and the claim that each beat matters is only convincing once you have removed one yourself.

Three rules make an interactive teach rather than decorate:

  • The default state already teaches. Patel: "when feasible, start a diagram in a 'useful' state that doesn't require interaction to learn from it." The beat switches start with every beat on, so a reader who never touches them, or has no JavaScript, still reads the whole rewrite.
  • Say what to do and what to look for in the sentence before it. "Switch off the crack and read the fix again" is an instruction; "explore the demo" is not.
  • The caption says what to notice, not what the picture is, and what not to read into it. The beat switches' caption says their notes are a reading, not a measurement.

Your turn: a drafting routine

Here is the whole method as a routine for your next explanation: a blog post, a design doc, an onboarding page.

  1. Name the reader and the one question the page answers, with a number in it if you can. Leaving out fundamentals that reader already has is correct, not careless.
  2. Read two or three existing explanations and mark where you stalled. Whichever step stalled you gets a section of its own in your page. Note the wrong model your reader most likely brings.
  3. Pick one running example that keeps the hard part hard, from real data or real code where you can.
  4. Lay out the ladder: for each idea a need, a naive attempt, a crack and a fix, joined by "but" and "therefore".
  5. List your promises: every simplification and deferral, with where it is paid off.
  6. Place one or two predictions at the key step, built so the nearest wrong model gives a different answer.
  7. Draft the prose, add each figure against a named failure of the prose, and write the opening and the summary last.
  8. Get a cold read. Ask someone who doesn't know the topic where they first stalled, not what they thought of it.

The last step is the one the curse of knowledge makes necessary, because you can't read your own page as a newcomer. Ask the reader to read silently and stop at the first sentence where they lost the thread. When no person is free, this blog uses a fresh AI agent as a stand-in: given only the page and the reader's background, with no conversation history, it reports its first stall, every term it couldn't place, and every number whose origin it couldn't trace. It has read far more than any newcomer, so treat what it finds as a minimum, not the whole list.

Optional exercise: rewrite a clear paragraph about B-trees

Here is an explanation in the same textbook shape as Wikipedia's. I wrote it for this exercise, but you will find versions of it in many books:

“A B-tree is a balanced search tree in which each node holds many keys and has many children. Its large fanout keeps the tree shallow, which minimises the number of disk reads per lookup.”

It is clear and correct. Rewrite it with a need, a naive attempt, a crack and a fix, and keep the word "fanout" until the end. Open the hints one at a time, only when you are stuck.

Hint 1: what would a reader try first?

The need: look a key up among a billion stored on disk, where each read from disk costs far more than any comparison. What search structure would a programmer reach for first?

Hint 2: count what that attempt costs

A balanced binary search tree over a billion keys (about 230) is about 30 levels deep, and each level is a separate read from disk. Each read also fetches a whole page, typically 4 to 16 KB, only to use one key from it. Where is the waste, and what property must the fix have?

Hint 3: one worked answer

Need: a lookup among a billion keys on disk, where each read costs far more than any comparison. Naive attempt: a balanced binary search tree. Crack: about 30 reads per lookup, and each read brings in a whole page to use one key, so the fix must use the rest of the page. Fix: fill each node with as many keys as a page holds, a few hundred, so one read narrows the search a few-hundred-fold, and a billion keys need about 4 levels. Name: the number of children per node is the fanout, and a tree built this way is a B-tree.

When the loop is the wrong tool

Everything so far assumes a reader who has to learn the idea. But the loop spends words to buy understanding, and not every reader wants to buy it.

  • Reference material. A reader looking up a field width wants the table, not a story. Put specifications, full tables and edge cases after the narrative or on a page of their own, as this blog does with the DEFLATE format reference.
  • Readers who need the decision, not the idea. Someone approving a design wants the proposal first. This blog's design-doc template puts the proposed design before the alternatives for that reason. Even then, each section should open by saying where it is going.
  • Readers who already know. Help that a newcomer needs slows an expert down, measurably; the research calls it the expertise reversal effect (Kalyuga and colleagues, 2003). Give experts a visible way past the scaffolding, as the line under this page's summary does.
  • Wrong models your reader doesn't hold. Refuting a misconception works when the reader holds it. Refuting one they don't is a detour. Bartosz Ciechanowski's Airfoil builds the right model of lift and leaves the popular wrong explanations to its further reading.
  • Interesting extras. Anecdotes and history added as colour compete with the idea for the reader's attention. Muller and Sharma tested this too: an "Extended Exposition" with "additional interesting information" did no better than the plain lecture. Put extras in a sidenote, or after the payoff.
  • Your own aha. The analogy that finally made an idea click for you is the residue of hours spent with its details. Handed over without the details, it explains nothing. Brent Yorgey named this the monad tutorial fallacy: "failing to recognize the critical role that struggling through fundamental details plays in the building of intuition." Give the details and examples, and the analogy at the end, as a summary.
  • A toy that teaches the wrong thing. Sanderson traced much of the confusion over his video on Grover's algorithm to an example. "This is almost certainly exacerbated by me briefly flashing an example function that just checks if the input is 12", which made the function look as if it already knew the answer. Ask what a literal reader would infer from your example.

Back to the book on the ramp

The question was what to change in a clear, correct explanation that a reader feels good about and cannot use.

The student on the ramp had a model before the video started: something has to win for the book to keep moving. The clear video never mentioned that model, so nothing in it collided with it. She heard true sentences, fitted them around what she already believed, and felt she understood better. The Dialogue said her model out loud and broke it on a case. That is a naive attempt and a crack, and it doubled what students learned.

So what we change is not the clarity. We change the order, and we ask for commitment. Give the reader the question before the answer and their own wrong answer before the right one; break the wrong answer where the break points at the fix; name the fix last; then ask a question their old model gets wrong. The Wikipedia paragraph needed nothing new for any of this except a count, the reader's own wrong answer, a check and a new order. Here is the whole method in five lines, the four beats and the check:

  1. Need: a question, with numbers, that the reader wants answered.
  2. Naive attempt: the obvious answer, tried concretely and labelled as a first try.
  3. Crack: where it breaks, phrased as the property any fix must have.
  4. Fix, then name: the fix in the words of that property, then its technical name.
  5. Check: a question the reader's old model answers differently.

Nothing here is specific to prose. This blog's template for decision records runs the loop by construction: the context is the need, each option considered is an attempt with its drawbacks as its crack, and the decision is the fix. So does a review comment that shows the failing input before the suggested change, or a commit message that says what broke before what changed.

Reach for the loop when you catch yourself writing the answer first: a definition, a rule, a "simply". Reach for a check whenever a reader tells you an explanation was clear. As the ramp showed, that is the one thing a reader can say whether or not they learned.

Two cautions on how far to trust all this. The evidence comes mostly from short lessons with novices, physics students in the clearest case, tested soon afterwards; the effects are moderate, not miracles. And the core of the method comes from one explainer's habits, Grant Sanderson's, found by counting simple features across 118 of his scripts (sentence length, how often he says "you", how often he asks the viewer to predict) and cross-checked against other writers and the research. Treat it as a strong default, and check it against your own readers.
